# <span class="chapnum">Chapter 15</span>Grating Couplers

A grating etched into the waveguide diffracts light up towards a fibre, so a chip can be tested anywhere on the wafer.
{: .chapterlead }

<div class="tbox tbox-objectives" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">In this chapter you will learn to</div>

- write the phase-matching condition and draw its wavevector diagram;
- relate period, angle and wavelength, and choose a period;
- explain directionality and the role of the buried-oxide thickness;
- estimate coupling efficiency from directionality and mode overlap, and the bandwidth from the fibre's angular acceptance;
- explain back-reflection fringes, and describe apodised, focusing, SWG and 2D grating couplers.

</div>

<div class="tbox tbox-insight" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Design question</div>

Which period and angle couple 1550 nm TE light into a fibre tilted at 8°, and how much of the light can you expect to get?

</div>

## <span class="secnum">15.1</span>Phase matching

A silicon waveguide's mode is 0.5 µm wide; a single-mode fibre's is 10 µm wide and arrives from above. A **grating coupler** bridges the two in one step. The waveguide is first widened to about 12 µm, so that the mode is as wide as the fibre's, and then runs into a shallow periodic etch. Each tooth scatters a little light, and the scattered waves add up in one direction, the direction in which their phases agree ([Figure 15.1](#fig-15-1)).

<figure id="fig-15-1">

  <figcaption><span class="capnum">Figure 15.1.</span> <strong>A grating coupler to scale.</strong> (a) Side view along the waveguide: 220 nm silicon with a 70 nm shallow etch, a 2 µm BOX on the silicon substrate, an oxide cladding (drawn 2 µm thick) and SMF-28 (core 8.2 µm, cladding 125 µm {% include cite.html keys="itug652" %}) tilted at 8°, its face 10 µm above the chip. The beam, as wide as the fibre mode and centred 3.8 µm from the start of the grating (Section 15.3), leaves at 5.5° in the oxide and refracts to 8° in air. Part of the light goes down, and the substrate reflects 17 % of it back up (Section 15.2). (b) Three periods of the 20 µm grating (33 periods): Λ = 607 nm and a 50 % fill factor (Worked Example 15.1). Both panels to scale. Drawn by <code>figsrc/ch15_figs.py</code>.</figcaption>
</figure>

Take the grating along $$z$$ with period $$\Lambda$$, and let the guided mode in the grating region have effective index $$n_\mathrm{eff}$$. The periodicity can add or remove whole multiples of the **grating vector** $$K = 2\pi/\Lambda$$ to the mode's propagation constant. A wave leaves the grating at angle $$\theta$$ to the normal into a cladding of index $$n_c$$ when its component along $$z$$ matches, $$k_0n_c\sin\theta = \beta - K$$, for the first diffraction order. Dividing by $$k_0 = 2\pi/\lambda$$:

<div class="tbox tbox-keyeq" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Grating-coupler phase matching (first order)</div>

$$n_\mathrm{eff} - n_c\sin\theta_c = \frac{\lambda}{\Lambda}, \qquad n_c\sin\theta_c = \sin\theta_\mathrm{air}$$

</div>

The second relation is Snell's law at the flat top of the cladding: whatever the cladding, the condition can be written with the fibre angle in air. The **wavevector diagram** ([Figure 15.2](#fig-15-2)) draws the same thing: an arrow of length $$n_\mathrm{eff}$$ along the waveguide, a step $$\lambda/\Lambda$$ back, and the result must land inside the circle of radius $$n_c$$ for the light to escape upwards. It lands inside the oxide circle below just as well, so some light always goes down into the buried oxide (Section 15.2). The grating's $$n_\mathrm{eff}$$ lies between those of the unetched and etched slabs ([Chapter 5]({% link chapters/05_slab.md %})); for a first estimate it is their average, weighted by the **fill factor** (the unetched fraction of each period).

<figure id="fig-15-2">

Right: for vertical coupling the first order points straight up and down and a second grating vector lands on the backward guided mode">
  <figcaption><span class="capnum">Figure 15.2.</span> <strong>Wavevector diagrams, in units of \(k_0\).</strong> (a) The coupler of Worked Example 15.1: \(\beta/k_0 = 2.694\) less \(K/k_0 = \lambda/\Lambda = 2.554\) (Λ = 607 nm) leaves \(k_z/k_0 = 0.139\). Where the dashed line meets the circle of radius 1.444, light leaves upwards into the cladding at 5.5° and downwards into the BOX; in air (radius 1) the same \(k_z\) is the 8° fibre angle. (b) Vertical coupling, Λ = λ/\(n_\mathrm{eff}\) = 575 nm: the first order goes straight up and down, and the second, \(\beta - 2K = -\beta\), lands on the backward guided mode: the second-order Bragg reflection. Drawn by <code>figsrc/ch15_figs.py</code>.</figcaption>
</figure>

<div class="tbox tbox-example" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Worked Example 15.1 · The period for an 8° fibre</div>

In 220 nm SOI with a 70 nm etch, the TE slab indices at 1550 nm are 2.848 (220 nm) and 2.539 (150 nm), so a 50 % fill factor gives $$n_\mathrm{eff} \approx 2.694$$. With the fibre tilted at 8° in air, $$\sin 8° = 0.139$$ and

$$\Lambda = \frac{\lambda}{n_\mathrm{eff} - \sin\theta} = \frac{1.55}{2.694 - 0.139} = 0.607\ \mu\mathrm{m}.$$

The averaged index is only an estimate, but here a good one: a converged 2D FDTD simulation of the same 607 nm grating puts its peak within a nanometre of 1550 nm ([Lab 6]({% link labs/lab06_gc_meep.md %})). In an oxide cladding the beam leaves the grating at only 5.5°, which Snell's law turns into 8° in air.

</div>

Why not couple straight up, at $$\theta = 0$$? Then $$\Lambda = \lambda/n_\mathrm{eff}$$, which is also the condition for a **second-order Bragg reflection** ([Chapter 13]({% link chapters/13_bragg.md %}); [Figure 15.2](#fig-15-2)b): the grating reflects strongly back into the waveguide as well as up. Tilting the fibre by 8–12° moves the coupler away from that condition, and it is why fibre arrays for photonic chips are polished at an angle ([Chapter 17]({% link chapters/17_fiberarrays.md %})).

**Try it.** Change the period, fill factor and fibre angle; the side view shows the grating to scale with the fibre at its angle.

## <span class="secnum">15.2</span>Directionality

A grating in the middle of a symmetric oxide stack scatters roughly as much light down as up. The downward light crosses the buried oxide (BOX) and meets the silicon substrate, which reflects about 17 % of it ($$r = (3.476 - 1.444)/(3.476 + 1.444) = 0.41$$). The reflected light comes back up through the grating and interferes with the light emitted upwards. Whether it adds or subtracts depends on the round-trip phase through the BOX, so the **directionality**, the fraction of the emitted power that goes up, oscillates with the BOX thickness $$t$$ with period

$$\Delta t = \frac{\lambda}{2n_\mathrm{ox}\cos\theta_\mathrm{ox}} = \frac{1.55}{2 \times 1.444 \times 0.995} = 0.54\ \mu\mathrm{m}.$$

A simple model ([Figure 15.3](#fig-15-3)) treats the grating as a thin sheet that emits equally up and down above a reflector with field reflection $$r$$. The upward field is then $$1 + re^{j\phi}$$ times the emitted field, while $$1 - r^2$$ of the downward power is lost in the substrate. The directionality swings between 29 % and 71 % with the substrate's reflection. In this model the best BOX thicknesses are 1.89 and 2.43 µm, and the common 2 µm BOX gives 62 %. A real grating does not emit symmetrically, because its etch profile is asymmetric, so the true optimum is found by simulation. But the lesson stands: **the BOX thickness is part of the grating-coupler design**, and a coupler designed for one wafer stack may lose a decibel on another.

Designs that push the directionality higher:

- a **thicker grating**: a polysilicon overlay on the teeth, or a dual-etch profile, makes the grating emit more upward than downward;
- a **bottom reflector**: a metal layer or a Bragg stack under the BOX returns 90 % or more of the downward light; efficiencies around 1 dB or better have been reported this way {% include cite.html keys="marchetti2019" %};
- a **top reflector** for couplers read from below through the substrate.

## <span class="secnum">15.3</span>Mode matching to the fibre

Even with perfect directionality the light must also fill the fibre's mode. In a uniform grating each tooth scatters the same fraction of the light still in the waveguide, so the power left decays exponentially, $$P(z) = e^{-2\alpha z}$$, and the beam that leaves has the same decaying profile. Here $$\alpha$$ is the **grating strength**, the field decay rate, set by the etch depth and the fill factor. The coupling into the fibre is the overlap of this emitted field with the fibre's Gaussian mode ([Chapter 3]({% link chapters/03_gaussian.md %})):

<div class="tbox tbox-keyeq" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Coupling efficiency of a grating coupler</div>

$$\eta = D \cdot \left\lvert \int \sqrt{2\alpha(z)}\,e^{-\int_0^z \alpha\,\mathrm{d}z'}\;g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z \right\rvert^2$$

with $$D$$ the directionality and $$g(z)$$ the fibre's normalised mode field projected on the grating.

</div>

No exponential matches a Gaussian well. Optimising the strength and the fibre position ([Figure 15.4](#fig-15-4)) gives at best about **80 % overlap** (−0.96 dB) for a fibre mode of waist $$w_0 = 5.2$$ µm. That needs $$1/\alpha \approx 1.46\,w_0 = 7.6$$ µm, with the fibre about 3.8 µm from the start of the grating {% include cite.html keys="taillaert2006" %}. A weaker grating spreads the light over more than the fibre's mode and lets light pass the end unused; a stronger one emits a spot that is too small.

<figure id="fig-15-4">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/figs/ch15_overlap.svg' | relative_url }}" style="width:600px" alt="Left: power emitted per micrometre along a uniform grating for grating strengths with 1 over alpha of 2.6, 7.6 and 20.8 micrometres, compared with the Gaussian fibre mode centred 3.8 micrometres from the start. Right: best overlap against 1 over alpha, peaking at 80 percent at 7.6 micrometres, with a dashed line at 95 percent for apodised gratings">
  <figcaption><span class="capnum">Figure 15.4.</span> <strong>Mode matching of a uniform grating.</strong> (a) Power emitted per micrometre, \(2\alpha e^{-2\alpha z}\), for three grating strengths, against the SMF-28 mode (\(w_0\) = 5.2 µm) placed 3.8 µm from the start of the grating. (b) The best overlap for each strength, with the fibre position optimised and a grating long enough to emit all the light: at most 80 %, at \(1/\alpha\) = 7.6 µm = 1.46 \(w_0\). Too strong (\(1/\alpha\) = 2.6 µm) gives 59 % and too weak (20.8 µm) 63 %; apodised gratings exceed 95 % (dashed). Drawn by <code>figsrc/ch15_figs.py</code>.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Apodisation** removes this limit. Making the grating weak at the start and stronger along its length, by varying the fill factor or the etch, shapes the emitted beam into a Gaussian. The local strength needed to emit a target profile $$G(z)$$ is $$\alpha(z) = G(z)/(2[1 - \int_0^z G\,\mathrm{d}z'])$$. Overlaps above 95 % follow, limited by the weakest and the strongest grating that can be made.

**Try it.** Change the grating strength, the fibre position and the BOX thickness, then apodise the grating or put a mirror under the BOX. The side view shows the teeth deepen along an apodised grating, and the light going up and down.

## <span class="secnum">15.4</span>Bandwidth and polarisation

Phase matching holds exactly at one wavelength for a given angle. At another wavelength the diffracted beam swings away from the fibre axis. Differentiating the phase-matching condition, including the dispersion of $$n_\mathrm{eff}$$, gives

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = -\frac{n_g - \sin\theta}{\lambda\cos\theta},$$

with $$n_g$$ the group index of the grating region. The fibre accepts a beam tilted by $$\Delta\theta$$ with efficiency $$\exp[-(\pi w_0\Delta\theta/\lambda)^2]$$, which falls by 1 dB at $$\Delta\theta = 2.6°$$ for $$w_0 = 5.2$$ µm. With $$n_g \approx 3.5$$ this gives a 1 dB bandwidth of about 42 nm ([Figure 15.5](#fig-15-5)). Measured couplers are narrower, typically 30–40 nm, because their beam is not a perfect Gaussian and their directionality and strength also change with wavelength. The formula shows the knobs: a lower group index (as in silicon nitride or SWG gratings) or a smaller fibre mode widens the band.

Right: 1 dB bandwidth against the grating's group index for SMF-28 and for a 4 micrometre mode, falling as the group index rises, with the silicon grating at 3.5 giving 42 nanometres">

Grating couplers are strongly **polarisation-dependent**. TE and TM modes have different effective indices, so a grating phase-matched for TE at 1550 nm couples TM at a quite different wavelength or angle, and hardly at all at 1550 nm. PDKs offer separate TE and TM couplers; in the lab, a polarisation controller or polarisation-maintaining fibre sets the input ([Chapter 36]({% link chapters/36_testing.md %})); and in products, 2D gratings handle both (Section 15.6).

## <span class="secnum">15.5</span>Back-reflection

Every grating reflects a little light back into the waveguide, from the abrupt start of the teeth and from the residual second-order Bragg reflection. Two couplers on the same waveguide then form a Fabry–Pérot cavity, and a measured spectrum shows **fringes** spaced by $$\lambda^2/(2n_gL)$$ ([Figure 15.6](#fig-15-6)). For couplers 1 mm apart that is 0.29 nm. If each coupler reflects −20 dB, the fringes are 0.17 dB from peak to trough; at −15 dB they reach 0.55 dB, enough to blur the resonance of a ring or the edge of a filter under test. Back-reflection is worse for a laser on the chip, whose linewidth and stability suffer from feedback ([Chapter 25]({% link chapters/25_dynamics.md %})). Couplers with engineered first teeth, curved lines and SWG teeth bring the reflection to below −20 dB {% include cite.html keys="wang2014gc" %}.

## <span class="secnum">15.6</span>Focusing, SWG and 2D grating couplers

- **Focusing couplers.** Instead of a straight grating fed by a taper several hundred micrometres long, curve the teeth along confocal ellipses so that the grating itself focuses the light into the narrow waveguide ([Figure 15.7](#fig-15-7)). The coupler becomes several times shorter with no loss of efficiency {% include cite.html keys="vanlaere2007" %}. Many PDK couplers are focusing, like the SiEPIC TE coupler and the Luxtelligence TFLN coupler in the [3D gallery]({% link devices-3d.md %}#dev-siepic-gc-te1550).
- **SWG couplers.** The shallow etch needs a second lithography step. Replacing it with a full etch whose teeth are sub-wavelength gratings ([Chapter 13]({% link chapters/13_bragg.md %})) sets the effective index by the fill factor and makes the coupler with the single etch that defines the waveguides. This is ideal for rapid prototyping by electron-beam lithography {% include cite.html keys="wang2014gc" %}.
- **2D couplers.** A grating periodic in two directions couples the two polarisations of the fibre into two orthogonal waveguides, both in TE: a polarisation splitter and coupler in one ([Chapter 14]({% link chapters/14_wdm.md %})) {% include cite.html keys="taillaert2006" %}.
- **Other platforms.** Silicon nitride's lower index contrast gives weaker, longer, more broadband gratings (the CORNERSTONE SiN elliptical coupler in the [3D gallery]({% link devices-3d.md %}#dev-cs-sin300-gc)); thin-film lithium niobate couplers are similar in principle, with the crystal's birefringence to handle.

## <span class="secnum">15.7</span>Grating couplers in practice

Grating couplers make **wafer-scale testing** possible: a probe station with a fibre array can reach any die without dicing ([Chapter 36]({% link chapters/36_testing.md %})). They need no facet preparation, they tolerate a few micrometres of misalignment ([Chapter 17]({% link chapters/17_fiberarrays.md %})), and they can sit anywhere on the chip. The price is a few decibels of loss, a bandwidth of a few tens of nanometres and a single polarisation. Edge couplers, the subject of the next chapter, make the opposite trade.

<div class="tbox-title">Your first chip</div>

Use the PDK's focusing TE grating coupler at 1550 nm, placed on the 127 µm pitch of the fibre array, with all couplers facing the same way. Add a **loopback**, two couplers joined by a short waveguide. Its spectrum gives the coupler's peak efficiency and bandwidth, and it is subtracted from every other measurement ([Chapter 37]({% link chapters/37_analysis.md %})).

<div class="tbox tbox-takeaways" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Key takeaways</div>

- Phase matching, $$n_\mathrm{eff} - \sin\theta_\mathrm{air} = \lambda/\Lambda$$, sets the period: about 0.61 µm for 1550 nm TE light and an 8° fibre in 220 nm SOI.
- Fibres are tilted by 8–12° to avoid the second-order Bragg reflection of vertical coupling.
- Efficiency = directionality × mode overlap. The substrate reflection makes the directionality depend on the BOX thickness, with a 0.54 µm period; a uniform grating overlaps a Gaussian by 80 % at best.
- Apodisation raises the overlap above 95 %; bottom reflectors raise the directionality above 90 %.
- The 1 dB bandwidth (30–40 nm) comes from the fibre's angular acceptance; couplers are polarisation-dependent, and their back-reflections make fringes in measured spectra.

</div>
