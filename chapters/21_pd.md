# <span class="chapnum">Chapter 21</span>Photodetectors and Receivers

At the end of every link a detector turns photons back into electrons; its responsivity, dark current and bandwidth set the receiver's sensitivity.
{: .chapterlead }

<div class="tbox tbox-objectives" markdown="1">

- compute responsivity from quantum efficiency;
- describe vertical and waveguide-coupled Ge-on-Si photodiodes;
- find the bandwidth from the RC and transit-time limits;
- include shot, thermal and dark-current noise, and avalanche gain;
- compute receiver sensitivity for a target bit-error ratio, and choose monitor detectors.

</div>

## <span class="secnum">21.1</span>Absorption and responsivity

A photodiode absorbs a photon of energy $$h\nu = hc/\lambda$$ and, with **quantum efficiency** $$\eta$$, delivers one electron to the circuit. The photocurrent per watt of light is the **responsivity**:

<div class="tbox tbox-keyeq" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Responsivity</div>

$$\mathcal{R} = \frac{\eta\,q\,\lambda}{hc} = \eta\,\frac{\lambda\ [\mu\mathrm{m}]}{1.24}\ \ \mathrm{A/W}$$

</div>

A perfect detector gives 1.25 A/W at 1550 nm and 1.06 A/W at 1310 nm. Silicon itself is transparent at these wavelengths (its band gap is at 1.1 µm), which is what makes silicon waveguides possible, so the detector needs a different material. **Germanium** is the natural choice: it is already used in CMOS processes, and its direct band gap (0.80 eV, about 1550 nm) absorbs strongly at 1310 nm and still usefully at 1550 nm. Germanium grown on silicon is under about 0.2 % tensile strain, from the different thermal expansion of the two, which shrinks the gap and extends the absorption into the L band {% include cite.html keys="liu2005,michel2010" %}.

## <span class="secnum">21.2</span>Germanium-on-silicon photodiodes

Germanium is grown selectively in windows on the silicon, and doped to form a PIN diode, in one of two ways:

- **Vertical PIN.** Doped silicon below, intrinsic germanium, and a doped germanium or polysilicon contact on top. The field is vertical, set by the germanium thickness.
- **Lateral PIN.** Doped regions on either side of the germanium (or of the silicon under it); the field is horizontal, set by lithography, and no contact is needed on top of the germanium.

Light reaches the germanium either from above (**normal incidence**), as in free-space and fibre-coupled detectors, or along a silicon waveguide (**waveguide-coupled**). A waveguide detector either butts the germanium against the end of the waveguide or puts it on top, where the light couples evanescently. The difference is fundamental. At normal incidence the light crosses the absorber once, so a fast, thin layer lets light through: a tensile-strained Ge-on-Si vertical PIN measured 0.56 A/W at 1550 nm against 0.87 A/W at 1310 nm, where germanium absorbs more strongly {% include cite.html keys="liu2005" %}. In a waveguide the light is absorbed along the length, tens of micrometres, so the germanium can be thin and fast and still absorb almost everything: responsivities around 1 A/W with bandwidths of tens of gigahertz.

Germanium and silicon differ in lattice constant by 4 %, and the mismatch leaves threading dislocations in the germanium. These raise the **dark current**, the current that flows with no light, to nanoamperes or microamperes, and they are what careful growth and annealing minimise {% include cite.html keys="michel2010" %}.

## <span class="secnum">21.3</span>Bandwidth

Two limits set how fast a photodiode responds:

- **Transit time.** Electrons and holes, generated across the intrinsic region of thickness $$d$$, drift out at about their saturation velocity, $$v_\mathrm{sat} \approx 6\times10^6$$ cm/s in germanium. The transit-limited bandwidth is $$f_t \approx 0.45\,v_\mathrm{sat}/d$$.
- **RC.** The junction capacitance $$C = \varepsilon A/d$$, plus parasitics, charges through the load and series resistance $$R$$: $$f_{RC} = 1/(2\pi RC)$$.

They combine approximately as

<div class="tbox tbox-keyeq" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Photodiode bandwidth</div>

$$\frac{1}{f_{3\mathrm{dB}}^2} \approx \frac{1}{f_t^2} + \frac{1}{f_{RC}^2}, \qquad f_t \approx \frac{0.45\,v_\mathrm{sat}}{d}, \qquad f_{RC} = \frac{1}{2\pi R\,\varepsilon A/d}$$

</div>

A thinner absorber shortens the transit but raises the capacitance, so there is an optimum thickness for each area. Smaller detectors push that optimum to thinner, faster layers. Waveguide detectors are small (a few micrometres wide and tens long), which is why they reach the highest bandwidths.

<div class="tbox tbox-example" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Worked Example 21.1 · A waveguide Ge photodiode</div>

A vertical PIN 15 µm long and 4 µm wide with 0.5 µm of intrinsic germanium ($$\varepsilon_r = 16.2$$) has $$C = 16.2 \times 8.85\times10^{-12} \times 60\times10^{-12}/0.5\times10^{-6} = 17$$ fF, plus about 5 fF of pads and wiring: 22 fF. Through 70 Ω, $$f_{RC} = 102$$ GHz; the transit limit is $$0.45 \times 6\times10^4/0.5\times10^{-6} = 54$$ GHz; together, about 48 GHz. If the germanium absorbs 3000 cm⁻¹ (an assumed value: near 1550 nm it depends strongly on wavelength and strain) and half the mode overlaps it, 15 µm absorbs 90 % of the light: about 0.93 A/W with 90 % internal efficiency and a little coupling loss.

</div>

## <span class="secnum">21.4</span>Dark current and noise

Three noise sources compete in a receiver. Each is a random current with a spectral density, integrated over the receiver's noise bandwidth $$B$$:

- **Shot noise** of the photocurrent and the dark current, from the discreteness of charge: $$\langle i^2\rangle = 2q(I + I_d)B$$.
- **Thermal noise** of the transimpedance amplifier (TIA) and its feedback resistor, quoted as an input-referred current density $$i_n$$ in pA/√Hz: $$\langle i^2\rangle = i_n^2B$$.
- **Relative intensity noise** of the laser and other optical noise, which this chapter neglects.

At the tens-of-microamperes signals of a short-reach receiver, the TIA's thermal noise usually dominates. Shot noise matters only at high powers or behind an avalanche gain.

## <span class="secnum">21.5</span>Avalanche photodiodes

An **avalanche photodiode** (APD) multiplies the photocurrent by a gain $$M$$ in a high-field region, before the TIA adds its noise. The multiplication is itself random, so it adds an **excess noise factor**:

$$F = kM + (1 - k)\left(2 - \frac{1}{M}\right),$$

where $$k$$ is the ratio of the hole and electron ionisation rates. Silicon's $$k$$ is small, about 0.1, much smaller than InP's. So the best Ge-on-Si APDs absorb in germanium and multiply in silicon (a separate-absorption-charge-multiplication structure). Beyond an optimum gain, the multiplied shot noise grows faster than the signal, and the gain–bandwidth product of the multiplication region limits $$M$$ at high data rates.

## <span class="secnum">21.6</span>Receivers

The TIA converts the photocurrent into a voltage, and a decision circuit compares it with a threshold at the middle of each bit. The noise blurs the "0" and "1" levels into two distributions. With Gaussian noise, the **bit-error ratio** depends only on the **Q factor**, the distance between the levels in units of the noise:

<div class="tbox tbox-keyeq" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Q factor and bit-error ratio</div>

$$Q = \frac{I_1 - I_0}{\sigma_1 + \sigma_0}, \qquad \mathrm{BER} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{Q}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$$

</div>

A BER of $$10^{-12}$$ needs $$Q = 7.03$$. Modern links run a forward-error-correction code instead, which corrects errors as long as the raw BER stays below a threshold: for the KP4 code used in Ethernet it is about $$2.4\times10^{-4}$$, or $$Q = 3.49$$, a 3 dB saving in optical power. The **sensitivity** is the lowest average received power that meets the target.

<div class="tbox tbox-example" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Worked Example 21.2 · The reference-link receiver</div>

A waveguide Ge PIN with $$\mathcal{R} = 0.9$$ A/W feeds a TIA with $$i_n = 20$$ pA/√Hz. At 50 Gb/s the noise bandwidth is about $$0.7 \times 50 = 35$$ GHz, so $$\sigma = 20\times10^{-12}\sqrt{35\times10^9} = 3.7$$ µA. For $$Q = 7.03$$ with thermal noise alone, the OMA must give $$I_1 - I_0 = 2Q\sigma = 52$$ µA, an OMA of 58 µW. With the 4 dB extinction of the ring modulator ([Chapter 20]({% link chapters/20_modulators.md %})), the OMA is 0.86 times the average power. The average must therefore be 68 µW, or −11.7 dBm; with shot and dark-current noise added, the demo gives −11.6 dBm. That meets the target of −12 dBm only approximately, and the reference link delivers −2.7 dBm ([Chapter 1]({% link chapters/01_why.md %})), leaving 8.9 dB of margin. Three levers improve it: a KP4 code moves the sensitivity to −14.7 dBm; a perfect extinction ratio to −15.0 dBm; a TIA with 10 pA/√Hz to −14.5 dBm. A Ge/Si APD at its optimum gain (about 13 in this model) reaches −19.2 dBm, though at 50 Gb/s its gain–bandwidth product would limit the usable gain.

</div>

## <span class="secnum">21.7</span>Monitor photodiodes

Not every detector carries data. Control loops need **monitors**: to lock rings ([Chapter 18]({% link chapters/18_thermal.md %})), to set MZM bias points, and to measure laser power. The simplest monitor is a weak **tap**, a directional coupler that sends 1–2 % of the light to a germanium photodiode. Where even that loss is too much, or space is short, **in-line** detectors absorb a tiny fraction of the light without a tap. Defect-mediated silicon detectors use sub-bandgap absorption at implanted defects, and the photoconductance of a doped silicon waveguide can be read directly. Their responsivity is low, but a monitor needs only a few microamperes, not gigahertz.

<div class="tbox tbox-specnote" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Reference-link choice</div>

Waveguide-coupled vertical Ge PIN photodiodes (about 15 × 4 µm, 0.5 µm intrinsic, about 0.9 A/W and 48 GHz) on each drop port, each with a TIA of about 20 pA/√Hz. Sensitivity about −11.6 dBm at BER 10⁻¹² (−14.7 dBm with KP4 FEC). Monitor taps on the drop rings' through ports feed the locking loops.

</div>

<div class="tbox tbox-takeaways" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Key takeaways</div>

- Responsivity is η λ[µm]/1.24 A/W: at most 1.25 A/W at 1550 nm. Germanium on silicon, tensile-strained, absorbs across the C band.
- Waveguide-coupled photodiodes absorb along their length, so they can be thin, fast and efficient at once; normal-incidence ones trade responsivity for speed.
- The bandwidth combines the transit limit (0.45 v<sub>sat</sub>/d) and the RC limit: about 48 GHz for a 15 × 4 µm, 0.5 µm detector.
- TIA thermal noise dominates short-reach receivers. BER = ½ erfc(Q/√2), with Q = 7.03 for 10⁻¹² and 3.49 for the KP4 FEC threshold.
- The reference-link receiver needs about −11.6 dBm at 10⁻¹² with 4 dB extinction, leaving about 9 dB of margin; APDs and FEC buy several decibels more.
