# <span class="chapnum">Chapter 24</span>The Fabry–Pérot Laser and the Rate Equations

Put gain between two mirrors, pump it until gain equals loss, and the device lases.
{: .chapterlead }

<div class="tbox tbox-objectives" markdown="1">

- explain the temperature dependence (T₀), the turn-on delay and relaxation oscillations.

</div>

<div class="tbox tbox-insight" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Design question</div>

What sets a laser's threshold current and slope efficiency?

</div>

## <span class="secnum">24.1</span>The optical cavity

The simplest laser is a semiconductor waveguide cleaved at both ends. The cleaved facets reflect $$R = ((n - 1)/(n + 1))^2 \approx 0.32$$ for an effective index near 3.4, and form a **Fabry–Pérot** cavity of length $$L$$. Light makes round trips; the cavity supports **longitudinal modes** wherever a round trip is a whole number of wavelengths, spaced by

$$\Delta\lambda = \frac{\lambda^2}{2n_gL},$$

0.83 nm for a 400 µm cavity with $$n_g = 3.6$$. Light leaks out through the mirrors at an average rate per unit length, the **mirror loss**,

$$\alpha_m = \frac{1}{2L}\ln\frac{1}{R_1R_2},$$

and is absorbed inside at the **internal loss** $$\alpha_i$$. A photon survives in the cavity for the **photon lifetime** $$\tau_p = 1/[v_g(\alpha_i + \alpha_m)]$$: a few picoseconds.

## <span class="secnum">24.2</span>Threshold

The laser reaches **threshold** when the gain over a round trip makes up exactly for the losses: the light returns to where it started with the same amplitude and phase. For the amplitude:

<div class="tbox tbox-keyeq" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Threshold condition</div>

$$\Gamma g_\mathrm{th} = \alpha_i + \alpha_m = \alpha_i + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\frac{1}{R_1R_2}$$

</div>

Above threshold the gain cannot grow further: any extra gain would make the light grow without limit, and the stimulated emission it causes pulls the carrier density back down. The gain and the carrier density are **clamped** at their threshold values, and every additional injected carrier becomes a photon.

## <span class="secnum">24.3</span>Rate equations

The laser's behaviour follows from two coupled equations, one for the carrier density $$N$$ and one for the photon density $$S$$ in the mode {% include cite.html keys="coldren" %}:

<div class="tbox tbox-keyeq" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Laser rate equations</div>

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\eta_iI}{qV} - R(N) - v_g\,g(N)\,S, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t} = \Gamma v_g\,g(N)\,S - \frac{S}{\tau_p} + \Gamma\beta BN^2$$

</div>

Carriers arrive with the current (a fraction $$\eta_i$$, the injection efficiency, reaching the active volume $$V$$), recombine through $$R(N) = AN + BN^2 + CN^3$$ ([Chapter 23]({% link chapters/23_gain.md %})), and are consumed by stimulated emission. Photons are created by stimulated emission and a small fraction $$\beta$$ of the spontaneous emission that falls into the mode, and escape after $$\tau_p$$. The gain $$g(N)$$ is the fitted logarithm $$g_0\ln(N/N_\mathrm{tr})$$, divided by $$1 + \varepsilon S$$ for gain compression at high power.

In steady state above threshold, $$N = N_\mathrm{th}$$ and the photon density grows linearly with current:

$$I_\mathrm{th} = \frac{qV}{\eta_i}\,R(N_\mathrm{th}), \qquad P_\mathrm{out} = \eta_d\,\frac{h\nu}{q}\,(I - I_\mathrm{th}), \qquad \eta_d = \eta_i\,\frac{\alpha_m}{\alpha_i + \alpha_m}.$$

The **differential quantum efficiency** $$\eta_d$$ is the fraction of extra electrons that become photons leaving through the mirrors. It is the injection efficiency times the share of the cavity loss that is useful output.

**Try it.** Change the current, the cavity length, the front-facet reflectivity and the temperature. The L–I curve, the switch-on transient and the spectrum follow from the rate equations.

## <span class="secnum">24.4</span>The L–I curve and efficiency

<div class="tbox tbox-example" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Worked Example 24.1 · A 400 µm InGaAsP Fabry–Pérot laser</div>

The demo's laser has a 2 µm × 50 nm active region ($$V = 4\times10^{-11}$$ cm³), $$\Gamma = 0.06$$, $$\alpha_i = 15$$ cm⁻¹ and cleaved facets. Then $$\alpha_m = \ln(1/0.32^2)/(2 \times 0.04\ \mathrm{cm}) = 28.5$$ cm⁻¹ and $$g_\mathrm{th} = (15 + 28.5)/0.06 = 725$$ cm⁻¹, so $$N_\mathrm{th} = 2\times10^{18}\,e^{725/1500} = 3.24\times10^{18}$$ cm⁻³. With the recombination coefficients of [Chapter 23]({% link chapters/23_gain.md %}), $$R(N_\mathrm{th}) = 2.4\times10^{27}$$ cm⁻³ s⁻¹ and

$$I_\mathrm{th} = \frac{1.6\times10^{-19} \times 4\times10^{-11} \times 2.4\times10^{27}}{0.8} = 19\ \mathrm{mA}.$$

Above threshold $$\eta_d = 0.8 \times 28.5/43.5 = 0.52$$, shared between the two facets, so the slope efficiency per facet is $$0.26 \times 0.80\ \mathrm{V} = 0.21$$ W/A. At 50 mA, each facet emits about 6.5 mW. With a 0.85 V diode drop and 5 Ω of series resistance, the electrical input is 55 mW, a wall-plug efficiency of about 23 % for both facets together.

</div>

The design levers follow from these formulas. A **shorter cavity** has more mirror loss: higher threshold gain, but less volume to pump, and a higher $$\eta_d$$. A **high-reflection coating** on the rear facet sends most of the light out of the front and lowers the threshold. **Lower internal loss** improves everything. And a smaller, better-confined active region with more differential gain (quantum wells) lowers the threshold current.

## <span class="secnum">24.5</span>Temperature

Threshold rises roughly exponentially with temperature,

$$I_\mathrm{th}(T) = I_\mathrm{th}(T_\mathrm{ref})\,e^{(T - T_\mathrm{ref})/T_0},$$

with a characteristic temperature $$T_0$$ of 50–70 K for InGaAsP lasers. That is the combined effect of Fermi smearing, Auger recombination and carrier leakage ([Chapter 23]({% link chapters/23_gain.md %})). The demo uses $$T_0 = 60$$ K: from 25 °C to 85 °C the threshold grows by a factor of $$e$$, from 19 to 52 mA. Self-heating from the drive current adds to the ambient temperature. When the threshold rises faster than the current, the output stops growing and falls: **thermal roll-over**, especially severe for lasers on silicon-on-insulator, where the buried oxide traps heat ([Chapter 27]({% link chapters/27_lasersonsi.md %})). The gain peak also moves, by about 0.5 nm/K, and with it the lasing wavelength of a Fabry–Pérot laser.

## <span class="secnum">24.6</span>Transients

When the current is switched on, the laser does not respond at once. First the carriers must build up to threshold, which takes the **turn-on delay**

$$\tau_d \approx \tau_n\ln\frac{I}{I - I_\mathrm{th}},$$

with $$\tau_n$$ the carrier lifetime near threshold, about 1.35 ns here, giving 0.9 ns at 40 mA. Then photons and carriers overshoot and ring as they exchange energy at the **relaxation-oscillation frequency** of a few gigahertz, before settling. These dynamics set how fast a laser can be modulated directly ([Chapter 25]({% link chapters/25_dynamics.md %})).

## <span class="secnum">24.7</span>The Fabry–Pérot spectrum

The gain spectrum is tens of nanometres wide, and the modes are less than a nanometre apart, so many modes see nearly the same gain. A Fabry–Pérot laser runs on several longitudinal modes at once, and its spectrum shifts and hops with temperature and current. Over fibre, the spread of wavelengths turns into pulse spreading through dispersion; in WDM, it wastes the spectrum. Single-frequency lasers add a wavelength-selective element, a grating or a ring, that favours one mode ([Chapter 26]({% link chapters/26_tunable.md %})).

<div class="tbox tbox-takeaways" markdown="1">
<div class="tbox-title">Key takeaways</div>

- Threshold: $$\Gamma g_\mathrm{th} = \alpha_i + \ln(1/R_1R_2)/(2L)$$; above it the gain and carrier density clamp.
- Modes are spaced by $$\lambda^2/(2n_gL)$$; photons live for $$\tau_p = 1/[v_g(\alpha_i + \alpha_m)]$$, a few picoseconds.
- $$I_\mathrm{th} = qVR(N_\mathrm{th})/\eta_i$$ and $$\eta_d = \eta_i\alpha_m/(\alpha_i + \alpha_m)$$: 19 mA and 0.21 W/A per facet for the 400 µm example.
- Threshold grows as $$e^{T/T_0}$$ ($$T_0$$ ≈ 60 K); self-heating causes roll-over.
- Turn-on delay and relaxation oscillations limit direct modulation; Fabry–Pérot lasers are multimode.

</div>
